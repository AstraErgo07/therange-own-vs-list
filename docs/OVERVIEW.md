# Own vs List (The Range, part of CDS): overview

Version v1.1. Generated by `tools/build_overview.py` from the code, at the default settings, so the numbers match the app. This is the plain-text version of the Streamlit app. Code: https://github.com/AstraErgo07/therange-own-vs-list.

Portfolio project. Made by Ami Sata.

## Purpose

For each product a marketplace partner sells on therange.co.uk (3P), should The Range buy and stock it itself (1P), leave it with the partner, or test first? The tool compares the contribution per unit of selling it 1P with the value of the 3P sale (commission less marketplace cost, plus the 1P substitute sales it does not take away), checks the return on the money tied up in stock, and ranks the products. The calculations are plain Python rules. A person accepts or overrides every recommendation. An LLM is optional and only drafts the explanation.

## Summary at the default settings

82 therange.co.uk listings: 47 sold by marketplace partners (the candidates) and 35 sold by The Range (used as the substitute a buyer might otherwise choose). Partner names are replaced with labels. Default settings: Bring in-house 21, Test as 3P first 8, Keep 3P 18.

Decision rules, in order:

1. If weekly demand swings by more than the volatility limit (50% of the average), test as 3P first.
2. If 1P and 3P contribution are within 10% of each other, or the return on stock is within 10% of the hurdle, test as 3P first.
3. If 1P makes more contribution per unit than 3P and the return on stock beats the 25% hurdle, bring it in-house.
4. Otherwise keep it with the partner (3P).

## How holding cost and the hurdle are split

The cost of holding stock used to be treated twice. A 20% holding rate (which mixed the cost of the
money tied up with warehouse space, insurance and shrink) was subtracted from 1P contribution, and
then return on stock, which is that same contribution for a year divided by the stock value, was
tested against a 25% hurdle. The cost of money was in both steps, so the in-house test was stricter
than it needed to be.

The two things are now split:

- Contribution only takes off the physical cost of holding stock: warehouse space, insurance and
  shrink. The default is 8% of the cost of the goods, for the days the goods sit in stock. That 8%
  is an assumption you can edit. Markdowns already have their own line, so they are not inside the 8%.
- The cost of capital is handled only by the hurdle. Return on stock is a year's 1P contribution
  divided by the average stock value, and it has to beat the hurdle (25% by default, also an
  assumption you can edit).

The 8% is my judgement of the physical part of the old 20% bundle, not a measured split. A firm
would use its own warehouse and shrink figures.

## Why the hurdle is 25%, and what a typical bar looks like

The 25% hurdle is an assumption, not a figure from The Range or from an industry rulebook. Firms set
their own bar, usually their cost of capital plus a cushion for risk. You can change it in the sidebar.

There is a related retail measure, GMROI (gross margin return on inventory investment): a year's
gross profit (sales less cost of goods only) divided by average stock at cost. A Shopify article
updated on 18 July 2026, which I opened, calculated GMROI from public company filings and the
figures ranged from 1.82 (Best Buy, consumer electronics) to 6.08 (Abercrombie & Fitch, apparel),
with Ulta Beauty at 2.36 and Home Depot at 2.23. Shopify does not give a single retail target; it
says a good GMROI depends on the category, and that a figure above 1 only means gross profit
exceeded average stock at cost. Source: https://www.shopify.com/blog/gmroi

GMROI is not the same as this tool's return on stock. GMROI uses gross profit. This tool uses
contribution after fulfilment, returns, markdowns and physical holding, so the figures are not
directly comparable and no published GMROI number is used as the hurdle. In this sample, listings
recommended in-house already show return on stock well above 100%, so 25%, 50% and 100% all give
the same recommendations. A bar in the region of 150% to 200% starts to change the answer. The
sensitivity table shows that.

Diversion is the share of partner buyers who would otherwise buy The Range's own item; COGS is the cost of goods. Changing diversion flips few products; moving the cost-price estimate by 10 points flips many. Supplier cost is the number to get first.

GMV is therefore not a useful ranking here; the ranking uses contribution per unit and return on stock instead.

## Limits

- A small sample of listings: a test of the method, not market sizing.
- Units come from a price curve, and the cost benchmarks are judgement, not CDS data. Every £ figure scales with them; supplier cost is the number to get first.
- The 8% physical holding rate and the 25% hurdle are assumptions. There is no single published retail bar used in the model.
- Where a product page showed a starting (From) price for a range of sizes or colours, that starting price is used.
- The Range's own craft kits in the sample sit at a lower price than the marketplace kits, so the 1P substitute contribution in crafts can be negative (cannibalisation is then floored at zero).
- The Range's pages were read with an automated page-reading tool, one at a time, no logins, robots.txt respected. What The Range's terms say about reuse of content is quoted on the data and method page.
